Title: Lost Technologies: What We Left Behind
Author: Marcus Webb, Survival Specialist
Body:
Before the Great Disconnection, humanity possessed technological capabilities that would seem like magic to survivors today. This article catalogs what we've lost and what we might recover.

The global internet connected billions of devices, allowing instant communication across continents and access to the sum total of human knowledge. That infrastructure required constant power, cooling, and maintenance that is simply impossible in our current state. The servers that once held the entirety of human knowledge now sit silent in dark data centers, their data slowly degrading.

Medical technology has suffered perhaps the greatest regression. Conditions once considered routine—appendicitis, infections, even dental cavities—have become life-threatening emergencies. Antibiotics, when found in pre-collapse caches, are treated with reverence once reserved for religious artifacts. Surgical procedures that were outpatient affairs now carry mortality rates exceeding fifty percent.

Manufacturing has returned to essentially pre-industrial levels in most regions. Without reliable electricity and global supply chains, even simple items like nails or screws have become valuable trade goods. The knowledge of how to create these items from raw materials is being painstakingly rediscovered by scattered communities of makers and engineers.

Transportation beyond walking distance requires either animal power or precious fossil fuels. Most vehicles that survived the initial electromagnetic pulse have long since degraded beyond repair. The art of maintaining internal combustion engines is dying with the last generation of mechanics who remember the old world.

Yet not all is lost. Solar panels, while degraded, still function in many areas. Shortwave radio operators maintain communication networks across hundreds of miles. Communities that preserved technical documentation through careful copying and apprenticeship are slowly rebuilding capabilities.

Perhaps the most tragic loss is institutional knowledge—the accumulated wisdom of how to organize large-scale human endeavors. Building a bridge requires not just materials and engineering, but the social structures to coordinate hundreds of workers over months or years. These skills atrophy quickly when survival demands individual focus.

Recovery, if it comes, will be measured in generations rather than years. Each preserved book, each maintained skill, each apprentice trained represents a thread connecting our present struggle to a possible future.
